1 · U.S.–China · AI + Critical Minerals

Sunday talks put tariffs, AI and critical-mineral flows on the same negotiating table

What happened: U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng are meeting in New York on September 20 ahead of the Trump–Xi summit. Reuters reports the agenda includes AI, tariffs and critical-mineral flows, with Washington pressing China over rare-earth supply commitments.

Why it matters: Any change in rare-earth availability or tariff treatment can move costs for motors, magnets, semiconductors, data centers, autos, robotics and defense supply chains across North America.

Who may benefit / hurt: U.S., Mexican and Canadian critical-mineral, magnet and component suppliers may gain if buyers diversify. Importers dependent on China-origin materials remain exposed to price and delivery shocks.

Action: Map rare-earth, magnet and China-origin dependencies in top BOMs and qualify North American alternates before the next tariff or export-control change.
Risk check: Talks are underway; no negotiated outcome should be assumed until the governments publish it.
Reuters · Sep. 20
2 · U.S.–Mexico · Trade

Mexico says bilateral trade negotiations are still moving forward

What happened: President Claudia Sheinbaum said Mexico is working to reach a U.S. trade deal soon after a productive call with President Donald Trump. Mexico continues seeking relief on U.S. tariffs affecting steel, aluminum and automobiles.

Why it matters: A bilateral package could reset landed costs and sourcing economics before the broader USMCA review is resolved.

Who may benefit / hurt: Suppliers with traceable North American content and flexible U.S.–Mexico production may gain. Import-heavy assemblers and firms dependent on unsettled tariff treatment remain vulnerable.

Action: Keep current-rule, partial-relief and tighter-regional-content pricing scenarios live for 2027 bids.
Risk check: No final legal text or tariff schedule has been released; political progress does not itself change customs treatment.
Reuters · Sep. 18
3 · Tariffs · Sanctions

New Russia sanctions law creates a second-order sourcing risk for the Americas

What happened: The U.S. law signed September 18 gives the president authority to impose tariffs of up to 100% on goods from major buyers of Russian oil or gas and certain sanctions-evasion facilitators.

Why it matters: An Americas manufacturer can face a landed-cost shock because of the energy policy of a supplier country even when the imported product has no direct connection to oil or gas.

Who may benefit / hurt: Regional substitute suppliers can gain share; importers exposed to designated countries could face abrupt tariff increases.

Action: Add Russian-energy exposure at the country level to supplier-risk maps and prequalify alternates for high-value imported components.
Risk check: Country designations and final tariff rates require implementation; the law does not mean every exposed country is already paying 100%.
Reuters · Sep. 18
4 · Dollar · Peso

Peso closes a difficult week with the official FIX at 17.2450

What happened: Banco de México’s latest official FIX is 17.2450 MXN/USD for September 18. Weekend market reporting put the peso around 17.22 per dollar, while banks were carrying Friday retail quotes because Sunday is not a Mexican banking day.

Why it matters: A weaker peso can reduce the dollar-equivalent cost of Mexican wages and rent while increasing the peso cost of imported equipment, energy and dollar liabilities.

Who may benefit / hurt: Dollar-paid exporters with peso expenses may benefit; Mexico-based importers and dollar borrowers face higher local-currency costs.

Action: Use FX bands, short quote validity, natural hedges and automatic currency-adjustment clauses.
Risk check: FIX is an official reference, weekend indications are not official FIX, and retail-bank execution rates differ.

5 · Energy · Logistics

Fresh Riyadh attacks keep Monday oil and freight risk elevated

What happened: Houthi forces claimed new missile and drone attacks around Riyadh on Sunday. Gulf markets fell while the region remains under pressure after recent attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure. Oil markets were closed Sunday after Brent settled Friday at $104.87 and WTI at $100.30.

Why it matters: Diesel, trucking, chemicals, plastics, asphalt and construction remain highly sensitive to any renewed threat to Saudi exports or shipping lanes.

Who may benefit / hurt: Producers, refiners and fuel-logistics firms can benefit from sustained high prices; fleets, manufacturers and fixed-price contractors absorb higher operating costs.

Action: Keep fuel-escalation clauses active for Monday quotes and monitor diesel separately from crude.
Risk check: Sunday attacks do not establish Monday’s oil price; de-escalation or restored export capacity could remove part of the risk premium quickly.

6 · Mexico · Industrial Incentives

PODECOBI turns Mexico’s nearshoring pitch into a tax-and-infrastructure package

What happened: Mexico’s official Polos de Desarrollo Económico para el Bienestar platform describes development poles combining logistics, public infrastructure and targeted tax incentives. The federal framework includes immediate ISR deductions for qualifying fixed investment plus 25% deductions tied to technical training and innovation/technology development.

Why it matters: Nearshoring site selection is moving beyond labor cost toward power, logistics, permitting, training and tax treatment.

Who may benefit / hurt: Manufacturers whose activities fit a designated pole can improve project economics; projects chasing incentives without firm power, water, labor or customers can still disappoint.

Action: Compare PODECOBI locations with established northern-Mexico clusters and Texas using total delivered cost, time-to-operation and verified utility capacity.
Risk check: Incentive eligibility is project- and decree-specific; confirm the exact activity, parcel, investment and deduction rules before underwriting.

7 · Trade Finance · South America

DFC approves a $500M trade-finance facility aimed at emerging-market buyers

What happened: The U.S. International Development Finance Corporation approved a $500 million facility with the World Bank’s IFC to provide counter-guarantees for U.S. exports into South America and other emerging markets. DFC says the structure could support up to $20 billion in exports and 10,000 U.S. jobs.

Why it matters: Payment and bank-risk support can make U.S. machinery, electrical products, metals and agricultural exports more competitive where local import financing is constrained.

Who may benefit / hurt: Smaller U.S. exporters and participating banks may gain new sales channels; competitors without comparable finance support can lose bids.

Action: U.S. exporters should identify South American distributors and banks that could fit the program and structure quotes around financed purchases rather than cash-only terms.
Risk check: The $20B export and 10,000-job figures are potential outcomes, not guaranteed volumes; the first participating banks and transactions still matter.
Reuters · Sep. 16DFC · board approvals
8 · South America · Energy Development

Suriname’s $12B GranMorgu project stays on track for first oil in 2028

What happened: TotalEnergies, Staatsolie and APA are progressing the $12 billion GranMorgu offshore project in Suriname, with first output targeted for mid-2028. Reuters reports roughly half of planned investment has already been used and additional exploration could support future expansion.

Why it matters: A new offshore production hub can create long-cycle demand for subsea equipment, FPSO services, marine logistics, engineering, fabrication and regional supply bases.

Who may benefit / hurt: Gulf Coast and Caribbean energy-service firms with offshore experience may gain; projects that assume immediate second-phase expansion carry timing risk.

Action: Build Suriname vendor maps around subsea, marine, inspection, fabrication and offshore logistics and watch qualification windows for future wells and FPSO work.
Risk check: Mid-2028 is a project target; offshore schedules, costs and future exploration results can change.
Reuters · Sep. 19
9 · Mexico · Nearshoring Reality Check

Record Mexican FDI still masks weak genuinely new investment

What happened: Reuters reported first-half Mexican FDI near $35 billion, but only 7.8% represented new investment, while 2025 greenfield investment fell nearly 50%. Companies cited USMCA and policy uncertainty as reasons for delaying some new projects.

Why it matters: Nearshoring is real, but expansion by existing plants is currently stronger than a broad wave of brand-new factories.

Who may benefit / hurt: Established industrial clusters with utilities and existing tenants can outperform speculative raw-land developments that depend on uncommitted greenfield demand.

Action: Favor phased capex, expandable facilities and sites with existing power, water and customers rather than underwriting only headline nearshoring forecasts.
Risk check: FDI totals include reinvested earnings and intercompany flows; do not treat aggregate FDI as new factory spending.
Reuters · Sep. 1
10 · Mexico · Fuel + Gulf Coast

Pemex refinery weakness keeps Mexico dependent on imported fuel

What happened: Reuters reported Pemex’s Q2 diesel output fell about 11% and gasoline output about 13%, while costly fuel imports rose sharply as refinery operations underperformed.

Why it matters: Mexico’s refining reliability affects trucking, manufacturing costs and refined-product flows from the U.S. Gulf Coast.

Who may benefit / hurt: Gulf Coast refiners, terminals, storage and fuel-logistics operators can benefit if import demand persists; Pemex finances and Mexican fuel-intensive businesses remain pressured.

Action: Monitor Gulf Coast terminal utilization, marine-loading activity, South Texas distribution and commercial fuel contracts tied to Mexican demand.
Risk check: One quarter is not a permanent trend; refinery utilization, maintenance and policy can change quickly.
Reuters · Sep. 17
