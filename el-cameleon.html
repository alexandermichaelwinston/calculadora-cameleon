Oct 30 · Canada duties

Canada's Section 338 tariff lists change today — product codes decide the bill

What happened: The October 23 White House proclamation changed the Canadian goods covered by an additional 50% U.S. Section 338 duty, effective for qualifying entries from 12:01 a.m. Eastern on October 30. Selected plywood, packaging and machinery lines leave the list while other goods, including some wood products and valves, enter it.

Why it matters: USMCA origin alone does not prevent the Section 338 duty on covered products; entry date and exact HTSUS classification control exposure.

Who may benefit / be hurt: Importers of exempted lines and some U.S. buyers may benefit; companies importing newly covered goods face potential higher landed costs, while domestic competitors may gain.

Business move: Run an entry-by-entry HTSUS audit of Canadian materials and request broker recalculations for shipments clearing October 30 onward.
Risks / verification: Confirm Annex I/II, country of origin, entry dates, Chapter 99 treatment, existing Section 232 duties and any CBP implementation guidance. This is not a blanket Canada tariff repeal.

Oct 28 · Robotics / Section 232

Industrial robotics import review enters negotiations — no new duty yet

What happened: On October 28, a Section 232 proclamation directed Commerce and USTR to negotiate with trading partners over robotics, industrial machinery and components, with an update due within 180 days. It did not impose an immediate new robotics tariff.

Why it matters: Factories buying imported CNC equipment, robots, controls and machinery now face a policy contingency that could reshape future automation budgets.

Who may benefit / be hurt: Domestic equipment and integration firms could gain; U.S. factories reliant on imported equipment could face later price pressure if new duties are imposed.

Business move: Create a dual-source machinery bill of materials and quote alternatives for critical robotic cells before signing long-term procurement contracts.
Risks / verification: Do not book a hypothetical tariff as current law. Monitor subsequent proclamations, tariff-code schedules, customs guidance and exclusions.

Oct 30 · Mexico macro

Mexico's preliminary third-quarter GDP rises 0.9% from prior quarter

What happened: INEGI's October 30 early estimate, reported by El País, shows Mexico's GDP up 0.9% quarter over quarter in July–September 2026. Industry grew 0.8%, services 1.1%, while primary activities fell 0.4%.

Why it matters: Improving output may support industrial leasing, exports and consumer demand, but the national aggregate conceals regional and sector differences.

Who may benefit / be hurt: Warehousing, industrial suppliers and services may gain from demand; farming-related businesses and weaker regional producers could lag.

Business move: Refresh market-sizing, industrial-park absorption and hiring scenarios using the new GDP estimate and monthly manufacturing/export series.
Risks / verification: This is an advance estimate subject to revision; confirm seasonally adjusted definitions and subsequent INEGI releases before presenting forecasts as facts.

Oct 29 FIX · USD/MXN

Peso weakens: Banco de México's latest completed FIX is 18.8148 per dollar

What happened: Banco de México published a FIX of 18.8148 MXN per USD for October 29, versus 18.6673 for October 28. The official FIX becomes known after midday Mexico City time on business days.

Why it matters: A weaker peso changes the dollar cost of Mexican labor and rent, while increasing peso costs of U.S. parts, equipment and debt service.

Who may benefit / be hurt: Dollar revenue earners converting into pesos may benefit; peso buyers of imported dollar-priced inputs and USD debtors may be hurt.

Business move: Reprice 30-, 60- and 90-day purchase orders using live executable bank quotes and quantify FX sensitivities.
Risks / verification: The FIX is not a guaranteed transaction rate; do not label the October 29 FIX as a live October 30 quote or assume future direction.

Oct 28 · U.S.–Mexico tariffs

Interim U.S.–Mexico agreement remains under discussion, not in force

What happened: Reuters reported October 28 that U.S. and Mexican officials are considering whether an interim trade arrangement could be reached ahead of the November 3 U.S. midterms; autos, steel and aluminum are important Mexican priorities.

Why it matters: Unresolved tariffs complicate sourcing, automotive production, steel purchasing and cross-border investment.

Who may benefit / be hurt: Businesses able to model alternative trade outcomes can adapt faster; importers assuming reductions before publication risk underpaying customs duties.

Business move: Build three landed-cost scenarios: current tariff schedule, partial sector relief and no near-term agreement.
Risks / verification: No signed text or revised tariff schedule was verified. Continue applying enforceable duties until official U.S. Customs instructions change.

Oct 29 · Reshoring

SEG Solar plans $950 million Tomball cell plant; 700-plus jobs projected

What happened: On October 29 SEG Solar announced a land agreement for a planned $950 million, 4-GW HJT solar-cell manufacturing facility near Houston. It expects more than 700 jobs and two 2-GW phases targeted for 2028 and 2029.

Why it matters: The campus could localize cell, module and component manufacturing and change U.S. solar sourcing and regional industrial demand.

Who may benefit / be hurt: Qualified plant builders, utilities, equipment and packaging suppliers could benefit; incumbent exporters may face stronger U.S. competition.

Business move: Track local permits and solicit vendor-prequalification details for clean manufacturing, process utilities, electrical, logistics and testing systems.
Risks / verification: A signed land agreement is not a completed factory or awarded subcontract. Confirm funding, project approvals and current domestic-content tax rules.

Oct 27–29 · Border trucks

Mexico's highway truck blockades lift, but freight security remains unresolved

What happened: Carrier organizations agreed October 27 to suspend highway blockades after protests across multiple Mexican states. Negotiations cover security, infrastructure, tax, social security and driver conditions.

Why it matters: Cargo may still face theft, insurance, inland-transit delays and reliability problems even if Texas border inspection lanes remain operating.

Who may benefit / be hurt: Insured secure carriers, cross-dock operators and tracking firms may gain; time-sensitive auto and produce shippers may face extra costs.

Business move: Check carrier dispatch, road warnings, bridge appointments and CBP lane data for each Mexico-to-Texas load; price secure parking.
Risks / verification: Do not describe all border crossings as closed or assume every corridor is clear. Verify specific routes and the status of promised working groups.

Nov 3 · FTZ

Two Texas Foreign-Trade Zone comment periods near closing

What happened: The U.S. Commerce FTZ Board lists November 3 deadlines for proposed production activity by Enerflex Energy Systems in Houston (FTZ 84) and a Perimeter Global Logistics subzone in Grand Prairie (FTZ 168).

Why it matters: FTZ procedures can alter cash flow and customs treatment for qualifying inventory and production, supporting industrial location decisions.

Who may benefit / be hurt: FTZ operators, freight forwarders and qualified exporters may benefit; unprepared competitors may overlook savings or incur compliance costs.

Business move: Review docket B-115-2026 and S-437-2026, compare duty treatments and submit evidence-based comments where directly affected.
Risks / verification: These are applications, not final approvals. FTZ privileges require authorization and CBP activation; Section 232/301 constraints may still apply.

Jan 12, 2027 · USMCA

USMCA's 2027 review invites operational evidence from companies

What happened: USTR opened public consultation October 2 for the 2027 USMCA joint review. Written comments and requests to appear at a hearing are due January 12, 2027 at 11:59 p.m. Eastern.

Why it matters: Manufacturers, border operators and suppliers can document real tariff, labor, rules-of-origin and crossing problems before policy recommendations are formed.

Who may benefit / be hurt: Firms with verifiable shipping and sourcing data can influence the record; unsupported lobbying claims or withheld evidence diminish value.

Business move: Collect anonymized shipment delays, invoice-level tariff costs, origin calculations and labor-compliance burdens into a concise documented submission.
Risks / verification: Consult the Federal Register docket and confidentiality procedures. A comment creates no automatic exemption, waiver or grant.
